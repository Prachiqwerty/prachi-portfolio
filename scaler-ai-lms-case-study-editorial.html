Problem definition

Status arrived without direction.

The audit produced four difficulties that followed a learner through the whole week, whichever surface they were on.

Opening the app

Status arrived without direction.

Locks, completion percentages, PSP scores, ranks and attendance reported where a learner stood. None of it connected to an action in that day's plan.

The learner had to translate a score into a decision, every session.
Planning the week

The structure mirrored the product, not the week.

Class, feedback, practice and progress were separate modules because they were separate teams. Learners were left to join them up.

Assembling the week became the learner's job before studying could start.
In the middle of a task

Context broke at the moment it was needed.

Scrolling past Calendar, Explore Scaler, Actions Pending and Practice meant rescanning each time. Inside a coding problem, the statement, the editor, the help and the submission history could not be held on screen together.

Every switch cost the learner the context they had just built.
When stuck

Help and personalisation had no continuous home.

Six parallel help systems ran in Problem Solving. On the home, static calendar entries never revealed why something was recommended, whether it was cohort work or enrichment, or where the Companion fitted.

A stuck learner had to choose a support system before receiving any support.

Exploration

Directions I explored and dropped.

The directions that did not ship explain the one that did.

Two directions for Path-maker reached a reviewable state before being dropped. Both were built to answer the wrong question, and both are recorded here with what was wrong and why it ended.

A

Path-maker as the full class list

Dropped

Path-maker opened as the whole program laid out class by class. Every phase expanded into its modules, every module into its classes, and every class into its primer content, live session, assignment and problem set. One page, one scroll, the entire curriculum visible at once. It looked right because it was complete, and because completeness is easy to defend in a review.

What was wrong with it

  1. It answered what is in this program when the learner arriving with 40 minutes was asking what do I do now.
  2. Every item sat at the same visual weight, so the page still handed over a sorting job. That is the exact failure the audit had just recorded on the old home, rebuilt in a new shape.
  3. It scaled with the curriculum rather than the session. Eight modules of classes meant the page grew every week while the learner's available attention stayed the same.
  4. Nothing in it was time-aware. A learner returning after two weeks away and a learner who studied yesterday saw an identical page.
B

The same list as a completion checklist

Dropped

The second attempt kept the structure and added state to it. A checkbox sat on every primer, class, assignment and question, and completion rolled up from item to class to module to phase. The reasoning was that if the list had to be long, at least it could tell a learner where they stood inside it.

What was wrong with it

  1. Checkboxes turned a long list into a visible backlog. For a learner already behind, the loudest thing on the page became the volume of what they had not done.
  2. Completion is not readiness. Ticking every box in a module said nothing about whether the learner could solve the next problem, which is what the program actually assesses.
  3. It moved the sequencing work onto the learner, when the system already held enough information to sequence the week itself.
  4. The interaction cost was high and the reward was a percentage, not a next action.

What both directions taught

Both failed the same test. They described the program instead of naming the next action, and both put the cost of that description on a learner who had already spent most of their attention elsewhere. The shipped version starts from the opposite end: one week, one clear next action, and the rest of the curriculum reachable rather than resident.

Key decisions / 01 · Path-maker

A Weekly Plan replaced the dashboard.

Later activities stay visible so the shape of the week survives without competing for the current decision.

Planning. What to do today and this week.

01

The Weekly Plan replaced the dashboard

Activities organise by date, priority and availability, narrowed to the current week.

The problem

The home showed the full curriculum and left the learner to work out which part applied to today.

How it solves it

Scoping the page to one week removes the sorting job at source. Everything beyond the week stays reachable rather than resident, so the curriculum can grow without the home page growing with it.

02

A Now marker and To-Dos for Today name one priority

A live Now marker tracks the current moment in the plan. To-Dos for Today carries the immediate set.

The problem

Calendar, contests, pending actions, practice and promotions all claimed the same level of attention.

How it solves it

Hierarchy is produced by time instead of editorial preference. Above the marker is behind the learner, below it is ahead, and the day collapses to the two or three items that are live.

03

Task cards carry their own context

Each card carries topic, type, instructor, duration, status, the reason it is in the plan and the next action.

The problem

Activity rows gave a title and a time, so deciding whether to open one meant opening it.

How it solves it

The open-or-skip decision now happens before the click. The card structure is generic, so onboarding steps sit in the plan using the same shape as a live class.

04

Locks explain themselves

Locked modules and the Careers Hub stay visible with the condition required to open them.

The problem

Locked modules and labels such as "AI Infused Learning 3/4/5" signalled restriction without cause or route forward.

How it solves it

A lock that states its own condition turns a dead end into a stated goal, so restriction reads as direction. The learner can see what the programme expects next without having to ask.

05

Companion sits beside the plan

Companion opens beside the plan with suggested prompts and carries the conversation without removing the learner from the task.

The problem

Companion support had no persistent place in the information architecture.

How it solves it

Asking for help no longer costs the learner their place. The task stays on screen and the suggested prompts remove the blank-page problem, so support arrives inside the work rather than as a detour.

Key decisions / 02 · Inside Class

One class, four connected moments.

Locked states state what opens next rather than only that something is closed.

Designed by Kishan. Recorded here because the surfaces share one strategy and ship as one experience.

Each session is organised around four connected moments: prepare, attend, practise, revisit. A Class Rail holds Pre-read, Live Class, Assignments and Practice in view throughout, and locked states state what opens next rather than only that something is closed.

Preparation teaches through familiar examples before short exercises let learners test the concept, with Companion available in the same flow. The lobby holds session outcomes and agenda while the primary action shifts from countdown to Join Live Class. After the session, recording, chapters, transcript highlights, personal notes, lecture notes, quizzes and flashcards stay attached to that class.

Key decisions / 03 · Problem Solving

Keep the whole attempt in one workspace.

A stuck learner had to choose a support system before getting support.

The attempt. Reading, coding, getting unstuck.

10

One workspace holds the whole attempt

Problem, editor, test cases, submissions and Companion share a single timed workspace, in light and dark themes.

The problem

The statement lived in the LMS, the code behind a VS Code launch, and help and submissions in mutually exclusive tabs.

How it solves it

Nothing the learner needs during an attempt is more than a glance away, so the cost of rechecking the statement or a previous run drops to zero. All Problem Space then pulls assignments, contests and mock interviews into one searchable entry point, so the workspace is reached the same way every time.

11

Six help systems became one path

Support escalates along a single path, from Companion hints through to a TA.

The problem

A stuck learner had to choose a support system before receiving any support.

How it solves it

Removing the choice removes the decision that was blocking help. Because the conversation is saved as the learner escalates, a TA arrives with a record of what has already been tried rather than starting from nothing.

12

Scored friction makes the cost of a shortcut explicit

Hints reduce the available score. Revealing the complete solution sets it to zero.

The problem

On a platform where the answer is always available, the easiest path is to read it.

How it solves it

Stating the cost before the learner commits turns an invisible trade-off into a deliberate one. The shortcut stays available, which matters for someone genuinely stuck, but it is no longer the cheapest route through the problem.

13

Submission history connects to the next attempt

Submission history separates Accepted, Wrong Answer, Time Limit and Compile Error, and sits beside the code.

The problem

The submission table recorded an answer and a runtime, detached from the error and the guidance used.

How it solves it

Keeping outcome, runtime and code in one view makes a failed run readable as a next step rather than as a score. The learner can see what changed between attempts without leaving the editor.

Learnings

What I took from it.

Static screens get approved on how they look. Prototypes get approved on how they behave, and behaviour is what ships.
01 / Structure

Product structure has to match the service model.

A static dashboard cannot represent an adaptive curriculum. When a capability defines the product, the information architecture needs a place for it. Without one, learners meet it as a feature and read the platform as generic.

02 / State coverage

Prototype the states, not the happy path.

Making all 16 personas and every empty, locked and error state switchable moved edge cases into design review while they were cheap. Static screens get approved on how they look. Prototypes get approved on how they behave, and behaviour is what ships.

03 / Method

Match the medium to the decision stage.

HTML for validating behaviour quickly. Figma for the precision handoff requires. Using either for the other's job costs a week.

04 / Adoption

Sequence adoption deliberately.

Introducing one unfamiliar surface at a time gives learners a stable reference point and reduces the cognitive load of platform-level change.
