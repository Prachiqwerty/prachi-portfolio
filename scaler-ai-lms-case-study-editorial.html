[ 01 / 06 ]Overview

About Scaler

Scaler is a technology education platform for working professionals, built around structured programs, live classes, mentorship and placement support.

Overview

Scaler moved its curriculum to an AI-native model. The LMS was built for the previous model and could not represent the new one, so we rebuilt the learner-facing experience around one clear next action per session.

My role

I was the Lead Product Designer, working alongside Kishan. I owned Path-maker and the problem-solving workspace, covering interaction, flows, states and brand application.

Scaler moved to an AI-native curriculum.

The LMS sits at the centre of a learner's week. They use it to find what to study, join live classes, practise, ask for help, and understand where they stand in a program they are paying for.

Working professionals

The LMS was built for a fixed curriculum and an unlimited attention span, so it could hold neither.

Sorting before studying

The home page carried Calendar, contests, pending actions, practice, promotions and performance data at the same level. A learner with 40 minutes spent the first of them deciding what the page was asking for.

Six ways to ask for help

Chat GPT Help, Use Hint, Solution Approach, Complete Solution, tutorials and TA requests each ran on different rules. A stuck learner had to pick a support system before receiving any support.

Personalisation surfaced as scattered features. A learner meeting them one at a time had no reason to read the platform as anything other than generic.

[ 03 / 06 ]Problem

Every surface reported status,
and none of it named an action

The audit produced four difficulties that followed a learner through the whole week, whichever surface they were on. Each session started with the learner translating a score into a decision.

Core problem statement

"Give learners one place that reads the curriculum for them, so every session starts with a clear next action instead of a list of things that are incomplete."

Four issues created this problem

What the audit found

I audited Path-maker and Problem Solving, and Kishan audited Inside Class. Each surface was read for two things: what was on the page, and what it cost a learner with limited time.

Constraints I had to work with

01

Attention is the constraint

Learners arrive in short, irregular sessions around a full-time job. Every screen had to earn its place in a 40-minute session.

02

Two owners, one experience

Two designers worked on the three surfaces in parallel, and I owned Path-maker and Problem Solving. The surfaces had to share one strategy and ship as one experience.

03

Learners already knew the old flows

Shipping every surface at once would stack three unfamiliar interaction models onto the same seven days, so the release had to be phased.

Three principles across the learner's week

Based on the audit, three principles ran through all three surfaces:

01

Name one next action, always.

02

Put the context for that action next to it.

03

Keep help inside the workflow, never one tab away.

The three surfaces in their final design, each built on the same three principles.

[ 04 / 06 ]Iterations

Two directions I explored
before the weekly plan

Two directions for Path-maker reached a reviewable state before being dropped. Both were built to answer the wrong question.

Path-maker as the full class list

Path-maker opened as the whole program laid out class by class: every phase expanded into modules, every module into classes, every class into primer, live session, assignment and problem set. It looked right because it was complete, and completeness is easy to defend in a review.

What was wrong

It answered what is in this program. A learner with 40 minutes was asking what to do now.

What was wrong

Every item sat at the same visual weight, so the page still handed over a sorting job.

What was wrong

The page grew with the curriculum, while the learner's attention stayed the same.

The same list as a completion checklist

The second attempt kept the structure and added state to it. A checkbox sat on every primer, class, assignment and question, and completion rolled up from item to class to module to phase.

What was wrong

For a learner already behind, the loudest thing on the page became what they had not done.

What was wrong

Ticking every box said nothing about whether the learner could solve the next problem.

What was wrong

It moved sequencing onto the learner, when the system already held enough to sequence the week.

Both described the program instead of naming the next action. The shipped version starts from the opposite end: one week, one clear next action, and the rest of the curriculum reachable rather than resident.

[ 05 / 06 ]Final design

The final design,
piece by piece

Path-maker · owned by me

1. A Weekly Plan replaced the dashboard

  • Problem identified

    The home showed the full curriculum and left the learner to work out which part applied to today.

  • What I did

    I scoped the page to one week. Activities organise by date, priority and availability, and everything beyond the week stays reachable rather than resident, so the curriculum can grow without the home page growing with it.

  • How the compression worked

    Four moves carried it: complete journey to current week, curriculum status to one clear action, generic activity row to context-rich task, and hidden dependency to explained unlock.

2. A Now marker and To-Dos for Today named one priority

  • Problem identified

    Calendar, contests, pending actions, practice and promotions all claimed the same level of attention.

  • What I did

    A live Now marker tracks the current moment in the plan, and To-Dos for Today carries the immediate set.

  • How it solves it

    Hierarchy comes from time instead of editorial preference. Above the marker is behind the learner, below it is ahead, and the day collapses to the two or three items that are live.

3. Task cards carried their own context

  • Problem identified

    Activity rows gave a title and a time, so deciding whether to open one meant opening it.

  • What I did

    Each card carries topic, type, instructor, duration, status, the reason it is in the plan and the next action.

  • How it solves it

    The open-or-skip decision now happens before the click. The card structure is generic, so onboarding steps sit in the plan in the same shape as a live class.

  • Scores with a next step

    A score never arrives alone. A contest at 42/100 carries Reattempt, an expired one carries Request Attempt, and a mock interview shows its days left beside Attempt.

4. Locks explained themselves

  • Problem identified

    Locked modules and labels such as "AI Infused Learning 3/4/5" signalled restriction without cause or route forward.

  • What I did

    Locked modules and the Careers Hub stay visible with the condition required to open them.

  • How it solves it

    A lock that states its own condition turns a dead end into a goal. The learner can see what the program expects next without having to ask.

5. Companion sat beside the plan

  • Problem identified

    Companion was a button in the problem screen header and absent from the home, with no knowledge of what the learner was working on.

  • What I did

    Companion opens beside the plan with suggested prompts and carries the conversation without removing the learner from the task.

  • How it solves it

    Asking for help no longer costs the learner their place. The suggested prompts remove the blank-page problem, so support arrives inside the work.

Inside Class

6. One class became four connected moments

  • Problem identified

    The class page opened on a Watch Recording button. Nothing prepared the learner before the session, and Assignment and Feedback sat behind locked tabs with no reason given.

  • What changed

    Each session is organised around prepare, attend, practise and revisit. A Class Rail holds Pre-read, Live Class, Assignments and Practice in view throughout, and locked states say what opens next.

  • After the session

    Recording, chapters, transcript highlights, personal notes, lecture notes, quizzes and flashcards stay attached to that class.

Problem Solving · owned by me

7. One workspace held the whole attempt

  • Problem identified

    The statement, help and submissions took turns in one panel beside the editor, and lab problems sent the code out to a separate VS Code launch.

  • What I did

    Problem, editor, test cases, submissions and Companion share a single timed workspace, in light and dark themes. All Problem Space pulls assignments, contests and mock interviews into one searchable entry point. The same layout carries code, frontend preview, VS Code launches, multiple choice, drag-to-fill and short-answer problems.

  • How it solves it

    Nothing the learner needs during an attempt is more than a glance away, so rechecking the statement or a previous run costs nothing.

One layout, six problem types.

8. Six help systems became one path

  • Problem identified

    A stuck learner had to choose a support system before receiving any support.

  • What I did

    Support escalates along a single path, from Companion hints through to a TA by text or video.

  • How it solves it

    Removing the choice removes the decision that was blocking help. Companion reads the failing test and earlier threads, so its first nudge is about this attempt. The conversation is saved as the learner escalates, so a TA arrives with a record of what has already been tried.

Companion inside the attempt, as the first step of one escalating path.

9. Scored friction made the cost of a shortcut explicit

  • Problem identified

    On a platform where the answer is always available, the easiest path is to read it. Hints were free and the complete solution cost half the score, so reading the answer was the cheapest route.

  • What I did

    Hints reduce the available score. Revealing the complete solution sets it to zero, and the cost is stated before the learner commits.

  • Why keep the shortcut

    Someone genuinely stuck still needs it. It stays available, and it is no longer the cheapest route through the problem.

10. Submission history connected to the next attempt

  • Problem identified

    The submission table recorded an answer and a runtime, detached from the error and the guidance used.

  • What I did

    Submission history separates Accepted, Wrong Answer, Time Limit and Compile Error, and sits beside the code. Any run can be compared with a reference solution, with a short note on why that approach works.

  • How it solves it

    A failed run reads as a next step rather than a score. The learner can see what changed between attempts without leaving the editor.

Work that went beyond
the brief

The brief was a home page and two connected surfaces. Four pieces of the work were not in it.

11. A journey model that became Classroom View

  • What I did

    Compressing a full program into one week's plan needed a map of the whole program first. I built a model covering onboarding, eight learning modules and career preparation across 14 visible states.

  • Why it mattered

    It made the compression decidable, then shipped as Classroom View: the whole curriculum, one toggle away from the Weekly Plan. The plan can stay short because nothing beyond the week is hidden.

12. Operational onboarding moved into the plan

  • What I did

    The brief covered learning activities. The manager call, batch allocation, mentor selection and Meet n Greet sat in a separate Actions Pending list. I gave them the same task-card structure.

  • Why it mattered

    A learner's first week now reads as one plan instead of two competing lists.

13. Every state made switchable before review

  • What I did

    All 16 personas and every empty, locked and error state were switchable in the prototype.

  • Why it mattered

    Edge cases reached design review while they were still cheap to change, instead of surfacing during build as engineering questions.

Four of the edge cases resolved in prototype review.

14. The prototype shipped as a handoff artefact

  • What I did

    I mapped interaction logic, learner states and edge cases in a working browser prototype built with Claude and Claude Code. Stakeholders approved direction from something they could click, and I translated the approved flows into production screens on Syntax by Scaler.

  • What changed

    Engineering normally receives a Figma specification. I shipped the approved HTML prototype alongside it, so interaction and state change did not have to be reconstructed from static screens during build.

What I would do
differently next time

  • Agree a retention measure before launch. With a retention measure defined up front, the first cohort could have shown whether a clearer week kept learners studying, which is the question the redesign was built around.
  • Match the medium to the decision stage, from day one. HTML for validating behaviour quickly, Figma for the precision handoff needs. Static screens get approved on how they look. Prototypes get approved on how they behave, and behaviour is what ships.
