Scaler Topics · Skill Test · Product design · 2024

1.1M readers a month, and nothing to show for it. A diagnosis sent 46% of test takers into a course.

Measured over the first week after release, across ~259 test attempts, when 35% of the people who opened a test’s detail page went on to submit it and signup through Skill Test ran at 7.59% against a 2% platform baseline.

Give a learner a diagnosis of what they actually know, then hand them the exact Scaler content that closes the gap. Twenty questions, two attempts, a 90% bar. Shipped in 20 days with one PM, four engineers and me.

Context & brief

First, a little context

Topics gave people plenty to read. It never told anyone what the reading was worth.

Scaler Topics had the traffic. It had no way to turn a reader into someone with a reason to come back.

Scaler is a technology education company running cohort-based programs for working professionals. Its learners range from non-tech professionals switching careers to senior engineers inside product companies.

Scaler Topics is Scaler's free layer: articles, free courses, topic hubs, tools, coding challenges, contests and masterclasses. It pulled 1.1M monthly active users in India. Most of them read something and left.

My role

I led the design end to end as the only designer on the project: discovery, information architecture, wireframes, visual design, prototype testing and the developer handover.

Problem definition

Nothing on the platform measured the person using it.

Topics could describe every topic it held. It could describe nothing about the person reading them. That showed up as four gaps, met on an ordinary visit, whichever article or course page a reader landed on, and met again by the business from its own side.

Discovery

What users asked for, in the order they asked for it.

People wanted the diagnosis first and the certificate last. Most assessment products present themselves the other way round.

We surveyed Scaler Topics users before any design work started, to test whether they wanted assessment at all. They did. The order they asked for things in mattered more than the yes.

1,200+users reached by the survey on Scaler Topics
222responded, an 18.5% response rate
171of them, 77%, said they wanted to know where their skills stood

Two inputs set the design

One from the people who would take the test, one from the products they would compare it against.

What 222 respondents asked for

Ranked by how often each request came up. This ranking set the order of the report screen.

  1. 01A real performance reportTheir own scores broken down by topic, rather than a single summary number.
  2. 02Show me against everyone elsePeer comparison and ranking. A score with no benchmark tells a learner nothing.
  3. 03Tell me what to study nextPersonalised recommendations tied to what they got wrong.
  4. 04Something an employer respectsAn industry-recognised credential, not a participation badge.

What HackerRank and LinkedIn already solved

The two products a Scaler Topics user already knows, reviewed in 2024. Three things they had right, all kept.

  1. 01A hard bar makes the credential worth havingLinkedIn badged only the top 30% of everyone who had sat that test. A certificate everyone gets is not a certificate.
  2. 02Verification is not optionalHackerRank issues every certificate at its own checkable URL. Without an ID an employer can look up, a credential is decoration.
  3. 03Scarcity protects itBoth cap attempts and impose a wait: thirty days on HackerRank, months on LinkedIn.

Users ranked the report first and the certificate last. Both products do it the other way round.

Bring users back to Scaler Topics by giving them a skill report on where they fall short, and how Scaler Topics can help them improve.

Their own catalogue. The HackerRank practice library after a failure, or LinkedIn Learning after an assessment.

The through-line

Both products are built to issue a credential. Skill Test is built to return a diagnosis, and the credential sits at the top of it.

Reviewed in 2024. LinkedIn retired Skill Assessments in 2026.

My approach

Three principles, applied to every screen.

Every outcome had to end with a next action, including the failures. Especially the failures.
01

Give a diagnosis, not a score.

A percentage tells a learner they failed. A topic breakdown tells them what to do about it.

02

State the rules before the test, never after.

Two attempts, a three-day lockout and a 90% bar, all on the screen before the first question.

03

End every outcome with a next action.

Including the failures. Especially the failures, because at a 90% bar that is most people.

What testing changed

The scoring system and the reattempt rules were the two places users got confused in prototype sessions. Both are the reason attempts remaining now appears on the pre-test screen and inside the failure state, rather than only in the fine print.

Information architecture

One path, with the failure branch designed as carefully as the success one.

Most users fail the first attempt. The product is mostly the failure branch.

A 90% bar means the majority of first attempts do not certify. Designing only the success path would have left most users at a dead end, so the branch below 90% carries the same amount of design work as the branch above it.

01Entry

Listing page, language course page or topic hub

02Pre-test screen

Modules, attempts left, the 90% bar, what you get back

03Assessment

20 questions, MCQ and coding, timed, weighted by difficulty

04Report

Summary, topic breakdown, Skill IQ, peer comparison

At the 90% bar, the path splits

90% and above

Certificate

Unique ID, emailed, downloadable, visible on the Scaler profile and verifiable by an employer. The user is pointed at the next level.

Below 90%

Resources, then a retake

Weak topics returned with mapped resources, a three-day wait, then a notification when the retake opens with a fresh set of question IDs. Two attempts in total.

Key decisions

The design, piece by piece.

Eight decisions, each recorded as the problem it answered and what I did about it.

01

Discovery states the offer before the click

A listing page that names the modules covered, the attempts allowed and what comes back.

Problem identified

Skill Test was a new object competing with articles, courses, challenges and contests. A test runs an hour and can be failed, which makes it the highest-commitment action on the platform.

What I did

Built a listing page that leads with the value proposition, then a grid of cards naming the modules covered with one clear CTA. Where a language has Beginner, Intermediate and Advanced levels, the card hierarchy makes the progression readable, so mastery looks like clearing each level rather than picking one.

02

The pre-test screen works as a contract

One screen carrying the modules, attempts remaining, the 90% bar, a certificate preview and a sample report.

Problem identified

Two attempts, a three-day lockout and a 90% bar. Discovering any of that after submitting a test would read as a penalty.

What I did

Every test opens on an information screen stating modules covered, attempts remaining and the certification bar, alongside the three things the user gets back: a timed test, a performance report and personalised recommendations. It also carries a certificate preview and a sample report, so the reward is visible before the cost. Accepting those terms up front is what turns the strictness into value.

03

The assessment shows its own structure

Timer, question navigator and flagging stay visible inside the existing test engine.

Problem identified

The timeline did not allow a new test runner, but the engine still had to communicate the logic underneath it.

What I did

Designed inside the existing engine and kept the timer, question flagging and skip behaviour visible throughout. The weighting is deliberately uneven, so nobody reaches 90% on easy questions alone. Retakes pull fresh question IDs, so a failed attempt cannot be passed by memory.

04

The report diagnoses before it scores

Your Skill Overview opens the report. The score follows it rather than leading it.

Problem identified

Users asked for a performance report more often than they asked for a certificate. A single percentage tells a learner they failed and nothing about why.

What I did

The report opens on the assessment summary, then moves straight into a topic-level breakdown of what went well and what did not. I introduced Skill IQ as the headline metric, because a raw percentage invites a pass-or-fail reading while a Skill IQ reads as a position on a scale. Answer review sits one level deeper, with the chosen option, the correct option and the explanation of the difference.

05

Peer comparison gives the score a meaning

The score read against the cohort and the top 10% threshold.

Problem identified

82% means nothing in isolation. The same score is excellent or mediocre depending on who else took the test, and Topics had no benchmark anywhere.

What I did

Placed the user's score against the cohort average and the top 10% threshold, with rank where available. The framing points forward, so a user above average sees the distance to the top decile instead of a congratulation that ends the session. This was the second most requested feature in the survey and it is what turns a report into a reason to return.

06

Recommendations attach to the question that was missed

A resource mapped to every question the user got wrong, free Topics content first.

Problem identified

Generic course promotion at the end of a test is advertising, and users ignore it because it is not about them.

What I did

Every incorrect answer carries a mapped resource. Miss the hoisting question and the report points at the hoisting module, not at a general JavaScript course. The content team maintains the mapping per question through the admin panel, typed as course or article plus a URL. Recommendations run free Topics content first and premium programs second, which is what keeps them credible. A user who scores 100% is pointed at adjacent tests instead.

07

The certificate is built to be checked

A generated certificate carrying a unique ID an employer can verify.

Problem identified

A certificate nobody can verify is a graphic. The competitive review put verification at the line between a credential and a badge.

What I did

Users at 90% or above get an automatically generated certificate carrying their details, the test topic and the date, with a unique ID. It is emailed, downloadable, visible on the Scaler profile and verifiable by an employer against that ID. The high bar is what makes it scarce enough to share, and sharing is the acquisition loop back to the listing page.

08

The reattempt wait becomes study time

The three-day wait, explained on the card before anyone meets it.

Problem identified

A three-day lockout after a failed attempt is friction. Left unexplained it reads as punishment, and it was one of two areas that confused users in prototype testing.

What I did

The failure state hands over weak topics and mapped resources immediately, so the three days have a job. Attempts are capped at two to protect the certificate. When the wait ends, the platform notifies the user by email and on-platform that the retake is open. Attempts used appears on the card itself as 0 of 2, and the locked retake button carries a tooltip explaining the wait, so nobody meets the rule for the first time after failing.

Above the brief

Three things the PRD never asked for.

The PRD described a listing page. It never described what that page owes a user who is already halfway through the product.
01

Card states that track where a user actually is

The brief specified a listing page and stopped there. Most users on any given day are midway through the product, so I designed the skill test card as a state machine covering the full lifecycle. Without these states the listing page would have told returning users nothing, and returning users are what the product depends on.

Not in the brief
02

A handover engineering could build without asking

I owned the developer handover rather than passing flat screens across. Twelve frames marked Ready for dev, every screen specified at 360, 720, 1024, 1280, 1440, 1920 and 2880, and components in a dedicated page so the card, report modules and recommendation blocks stay consistent as the content team adds tests. Ambiguity in a handover file gets resolved by whoever is closest to the deadline, and that is rarely the right person.

Two of the mobile frames from the handover file. Every screen in the flow was specified at 360 and 720 alongside the five desktop breakpoints, so nothing in the responsive behaviour had to be invented during the build.

Not in the brief
03

Feedback capture inside the flow

Question-level feedback and a feedback control on the report screen shipped with the first release. The content team uses the responses to find questions that are unclear rather than hard, which is the only way a question bank improves after launch.

Learnings

What I took from it.

Design the failure branch first. That is where most of your users will be.
01 / Structure

A strict rule needs a screen that explains it.

Two attempts and a three-day lockout are hostile rules if a user meets them after the fact. The pre-test screen and the card tooltip exist so that the same rules read as a standard. The constraint did not change. Where the user learned about it did.

02 / Sequence

Diagnosis before credential.

Users ranked a performance report above a certificate, which is the reverse of how assessment products usually present themselves. Following the survey order rather than the marketing order is why 46% of test takers reached a recommended course.

03 / Specificity

A recommendation is only credible at question level.

Mapping resources to the exact question a user missed costs the content team real effort per question. It is also the entire difference between a recommendation and an ad.

04 / Constraint

A fixed engine sharpens the work around it.

Being unable to touch the test runner meant every day of the 20 went into discovery, the pre-test contract, the report and the reattempt loop. Those are the surfaces that moved the numbers.
